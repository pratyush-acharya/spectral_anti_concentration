Robert Boyle was an Anglo-Irish natural philosopher, chemist, physicist, and inventor. He is
largely regarded as the first modern chemist, and therefore one of the founders of modern
chemistry. Among his works, The Sceptical Chymist is seen as a cornerstone book in the field
of chemistry. He was noted for his writings in theology as well. Boyle was born at Lismore
Castle in County Waterford, Ireland, the seventh son and fourteenth child of Richard Boyle,
the first Earl of Cork. After spending three years at Eton College, he traveled with a French
tutor across continental Europe, spending more than two years visiting various parts of Italy.
His father died in 1643, leaving him the manor of Stalbridge in Dorset and substantial estates
in County Limerick in Ireland that he had inherited from his great friend Sir Walter Raleigh.

The Amazon basin is the part of South America drained by the Amazon River and its tributaries.
The basin is located in the countries of Brazil, Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Peru,
Suriname, and Venezuela. The Amazon drains an area of some 6,300,000 square kilometres, or
about 35.5 percent of the South American continent. The source of the Amazon is the Mantaro
River in Peru, which merges with the Apurimac River and then becomes the Ene, the Tambo, and
the Ucayali before joining the Maranon upstream of Iquitos. Because of the vast forest cover
and the enormous amount of water flowing through the river system, the Amazon basin plays a
critical role in regulating global climate patterns and carbon cycles. The river discharges
roughly 209,000 cubic metres per second into the Atlantic Ocean.

The printing press is a mechanical device for applying pressure to an inked surface resting
upon a print medium, thereby transferring the ink. It marked a dramatic improvement on earlier
printing methods in which the cloth, paper, or other medium was brushed or rubbed repeatedly
to achieve the transfer of ink and accelerated the process. Typically used for texts, the
invention and global spread of the printing press was one of the most influential events in
the second millennium, revolutionizing the way people conceived and described the world they
lived in and ushering in the period of modernity. Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith by
profession, developed the first printing press in the Holy Roman Empire around 1440. His
introduction of mechanical movable type printing to Europe started the Printing Revolution
and is regarded as a milestone of the second millennium.

In mathematics, the Fourier transform decomposes a function of time into its constituent
frequencies. The transform is closely related to the Fourier series, which represents a
periodic function as a sum of complex exponentials. The Fourier transform generalizes this
idea to non-periodic functions by allowing the period to tend to infinity. The transform has
many applications in physics and engineering, particularly in signal processing, quantum
mechanics, and the solution of partial differential equations. It was first introduced by
Joseph Fourier in 1807 in his study of heat conduction, where he showed that arbitrary
functions could be represented by trigonometric series. The discrete version of the transform,
known as the discrete Fourier transform, is widely used in numerical computation and is
efficiently implemented through the fast Fourier transform algorithm discovered by Cooley
and Tukey in 1965.

Photosynthesis is a process used by plants and other organisms to convert light energy into
chemical energy that can be later released to fuel the organism's activities. This chemical
energy is stored in carbohydrate molecules such as sugars and starches, which are synthesized
from carbon dioxide and water. In most cases, oxygen is released as a waste product.
Photosynthesis maintains atmospheric oxygen levels and supplies most of the biological energy
necessary for complex life on Earth. The process involves two stages commonly known as the
light-dependent reactions and the Calvin cycle. During the light-dependent reactions, which
take place in the thylakoid membranes, water is split using light energy and oxygen is
produced. The Calvin cycle, which takes place in the stroma, uses the energy carriers produced
during the light reactions to fix carbon dioxide into three-carbon sugars.

The Byzantine Empire, also referred to as the Eastern Roman Empire, was the continuation of
the Roman Empire primarily in its eastern provinces during Late Antiquity and the Middle Ages,
when its capital city was Constantinople. It survived the fragmentation and fall of the
Western Roman Empire in the fifth century and continued to exist for an additional thousand
years until the fall of Constantinople to the Ottoman Turks in 1453. During most of its
existence, the empire remained the most powerful economic, cultural, and military force in
Europe. The Byzantine Empire is one of the empires that lasted the longest in history. Several
events from the fourth to sixth centuries mark the period of transition during which the Roman
Empire's Greek East and Latin West diverged. Constantine the First reorganized the empire and
made Constantinople the new capital.

The theory of plate tectonics describes the large-scale motion of seven major plates and the
movements of a larger number of smaller plates of Earth's lithosphere. The model builds on the
concept of continental drift, which was first proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912. The
geoscientific community accepted plate tectonic theory after seafloor spreading was validated
in the late 1950s and early 1960s. The lithosphere, which is the rigid outermost layer of
Earth, is broken into tectonic plates. These plates are not the same as the continental and
oceanic crust; rather, each plate is composed of both types of crust. The movement of the
plates is driven by convection currents in the underlying asthenosphere. The boundaries
between plates are associated with geological events such as earthquakes and the creation of
topographic features such as mountains, volcanoes, and oceanic trenches.

Antibiotics are a type of antimicrobial substance active against bacteria. They are the most
important type of antibacterial agent for fighting bacterial infections, and antibiotic
medications are widely used in the treatment and prevention of such infections. They may
either kill or inhibit the growth of bacteria. A limited number of antibiotics also possess
antiprotozoal activity. Antibiotics are not effective against viruses such as the common cold
or influenza; drugs which inhibit growth of viruses are termed antiviral drugs rather than
antibiotics. The first commercially available antibacterial antibiotic, Prontosil, was
developed by a research team led by Gerhard Domagk in 1932 at the Bayer Laboratories.
Alexander Fleming had earlier discovered penicillin in 1928, but it was not until 1942 that
penicillin was mass-produced and made publicly available for clinical use.

The global ocean covers approximately 361 million square kilometres and contains about 1.335
billion cubic kilometres of water. It is conventionally divided into several principal oceans
and smaller seas. The Pacific Ocean is the largest, covering an area of about 165.25 million
square kilometres. The Atlantic is the second largest at approximately 106.46 million square
kilometres. Ocean currents are continuous directed movements of seawater generated by various
forces acting upon the water, including wind, the Coriolis effect, temperature differences,
and differences in salinity. Thermohaline circulation, often described as the global ocean
conveyor belt, is a deep ocean current driven by density differences caused by variations in
temperature and salinity. These currents play a crucial role in regulating Earth's climate by
distributing thermal energy from the equatorial regions to the polar regions.

The Turing machine is a mathematical model of computation describing an abstract machine that
manipulates symbols on a strip of tape according to a table of rules. Despite the model's
simplicity, it is capable of implementing any computer algorithm. The machine was invented in
1936 by Alan Turing, who called it an automatic machine. Turing machines are central objects
of study in the theory of computation. The concept of the universal Turing machine, which can
simulate any other Turing machine, was an important step in the development of the modern
stored-program computer. Turing also introduced the concept of computability: a function is
computable if there exists a Turing machine that computes it. This led to the formalization
of the Church-Turing thesis, which states that any effectively calculable function can be
computed by a Turing machine. The halting problem, first proved undecidable by Turing, showed
fundamental limits on what can be computed.
